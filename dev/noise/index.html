Noise covers and biwhitening

The covers on the other pages are computed from the entries of a matrix. A noise cover is computed from the variances of the noise in a data matrix Y. Given a nonnegative matrix V of variance estimates, the same size as Y, noise_cover returns scales a and b such that the normalized data Y ./ (a * b') has noise whose variance averages one over every row and every column. This is the biwhitening of Landa, Zhang & Kluger (2022). After biwhitening, the singular values of pure noise follow the Marchenko–Pastur law, so components of the data that rise above the noise can be identified and estimated with standard tools.

What noise_cover computes

Let V be m × n, and let m′ and n′ count the rows and columns of V that are not entirely zero. Then

\[\sum_j \frac{V_{ij}}{(a_i b_j)^2} = n' \quad\text{and}\quad \sum_i \frac{V_{ij}}{(a_i b_j)^2} = m'\]

for every such row i and column j. A row or column of V that is entirely zero gets a scale of zero, as in every cover. The sums run over all entries, zeros included, so each row of the normalized noise has mean variance one across all n′ columns, not just across its nonzero entries.

using MatrixCovers, LinearAlgebra

a, b, stats = noise_cover(V)
Yhat = Diagonal(pinv.(a)) * Y * Diagonal(pinv.(b))

Dividing via Diagonal preserves any sparsity of Y, and pinv(0) == 0 provides the convention 0/0 = 0 for rows and columns with a zero scale.

noise_cover is related to, yet distinct from, soft covers with PowerMean:

  • In terms of variances, (a .^ 2, b .^ 2) scales V to the row sums n′ and column sums m′. The PowerMean{1} soft cover performs the same kind of scaling, but its targets count only the nonzero entries of each row and column. When V has no zeros the two coincide (up to the gauge a → t a, b → b / t); when V has zeros they differ, and the section on zeros explains why the difference matters for count data.
  • In terms of the noise E, (a, b) satisfies the balance condition of the PowerMean{2} soft cover in expectation, with every entry counted: the expected mean of (E[i,j] / (a[i] b[j]))^2 is one over each row and column.

noise_cover uses the solver of soft_cover with these targets: power-mean sweeps, Newton steps when the sweeps are slow, and the polish of weakly joined clusters. It accepts the same solver keywords (maxiter, newton, maxnewton, maxpolish, linsolve), and its stats is a PowerMeanStats. The Newton steps matter most for zero patterns that only barely admit a scaling (see zeros), where the sweeps alone converge slowly.

With shrink = 0, the cover is covariant: if V is replaced by Dr^2 * V * Dc^2 for positive diagonal Dr and Dc, then a → Dr * a and b → Dc * b. Rescaling the units of Y, and transforming V to match, leaves Yhat unchanged.

Building the variance matrix

noise_cover uses V and nothing else, so the quality of the normalization rests on V. It should be an unbiased, or nearly unbiased, estimate of the variance of each entry of Y, computed from the same measurements as Y.

Poisson counts. For counts with Poisson noise, such as UMI counts in single-cell RNA sequencing, the counts are their own unbiased variance estimate:

a, b, stats = noise_cover(Y)

Y must be the raw counts: values that have already been normalized, such as counts per million or log-transformed counts, no longer have variance equal to their mean, and passing them as V gives scales without statistical meaning.

Quadratic variance functions. If Var(Y[i,j]) = α + β X[i,j] + γ X[i,j]^2 with X = E[Y], then with γ ≠ -1

V = (α .+ β .* Y .+ γ .* Y .^ 2) ./ (1 + γ)

is unbiased. The negative binomial with dispersion θ has α = 0, β = 1, γ = 1/θ. MatrixCovers does not estimate α, β, and γ; Landa et al. choose them by fitting the normalized spectrum to the Marchenko–Pastur law.

Camera data. For a detector with gain g (digital units per detected photon), offset o, and read-noise variance σr² (in squared digital units), a pixel value Y has variance g (E[Y] - o) + σr², so

V = g .* (Y .- o) .+ σr²

is unbiased. Read noise can make a few entries of this estimate negative; noise_cover requires V ≥ 0, and clamping such entries (for example at σr²) biases them upward slightly. When Y has been summed over frames, sum the variances in the same way.

Differences such as ΔF. When the data are the change in fluorescence upon stimulation, ΔF = Fs - Fb, the variance is that of the stimulus measurement plus that of the baseline, each computed from the raw measurements as above:

V = Vs .+ Vb   # Vs from the raw stimulus frames, Vb from the raw baseline frames

The variance of ΔF depends on the brightness of Fs and Fb, not on ΔF itself, which may be small or negative where the brightness is large. A model fit to ΔF, such as the Poisson or quadratic estimates applied to ΔF, is therefore wrong for these data.

The independence assumption also constrains how differences are formed. If several rows of ΔF share one baseline measurement, for example a single baseline for each cell subtracted from its response to every stimulus, the noise in that baseline appears in all of those rows. The noise matrix then contains a rank-one term whose singular value grows like √(m n), far above the threshold √m + √n used below, and no diagonal scaling removes it. It will be counted as a component of the signal. Use an independent baseline for each row where possible; otherwise expect one spurious component for each shared baseline.

Other divisions. Dividing Y entrywise by sqrt.(V) does equalize the noise variances, but it is not a diagonal scaling: it changes the rank of the signal, which the methods below rely on. A diagonal scaling such as ΔF/F0, with F0 measured precisely for each cell, is harmless if V is divided by F0.^2 to match; with shrink = 0 the result is the same as without it.

Zero entries

A zero in V can mean two different things:

  • a structural zero, an entry that is absent, as in a contingency table with cells that cannot occur; or
  • a sampling zero, an observation of a small expected count, as in sparse count data.

soft_cover(PowerMean{1}(), V) treats zeros as structural: only the nonzero entries enter its targets. For count data this is the wrong choice. A Poisson count with mean λ, conditioned on being nonzero, has mean λ / (1 - exp(-λ)), which tends to one as λ → 0. In sparse count data most nonzero entries are 1 or 2 regardless of their expected value, so a scaling based on the nonzero entries alone barely responds to the intensity of a row or column, the noise variances remain unequal after normalization, and the Marchenko–Pastur law does not hold. noise_cover treats zeros as observations, which is correct for counts.

Counting the zeros has a cost: for some zero patterns the scaling does not exist. A row whose nonzero entries fall in only a few columns cannot have its variance raised to the target n′ without violating the targets of those columns. More precisely, after rows and columns that are entirely zero are set aside, the scaling exists exactly when some nonnegative matrix with the same zero pattern as V has row sums n′ and column sums m′. Roughly, every row needs at least n′/m′ nonzero entries and every column at least m′/n′, and groups of rows that share few columns need proportionally more. noise_cover checks this condition before iterating and, when it fails, throws a SolverFailure that names the rows or entries responsible. This is the counterpart, for matrix scaling, of the nonexistence of a maximum-likelihood estimate in a log-linear model when the table contains sampling zeros (Fienberg & Rinaldo, 2012).

When the scaling does not exist, there are two remedies:

  • Filter. Remove rows and columns with too few nonzero entries. Standard quality control in single-cell analysis often suffices.
  • Shrink. noise_cover(V; shrink=δ) with δ > 0 scales (V + δ μ) / (1 + δ) instead of V, where μ[i,j] = r[i] c[j] / s with r, c, and s the row sums, column sums, and total of V. For Poisson counts, μ is the expected count under a rank-one model. The shrunk matrix has the same total as V and is positive on every nonvanishing row and column, so the scaling always exists. μ is applied inside the solver and never stored as a dense matrix, so the memory cost stays proportional to the number of stored entries of V.

For Poisson counts, δ is measured in observations. Give each expected count X[i,j] a gamma prior with mean μ[i,j] and the weight of δ observations (shape δ μ[i,j], rate δ). Given the count Y[i,j], the posterior mean of X[i,j], which is also the posterior mean of its variance, is (Y[i,j] + δ μ[i,j]) / (1 + δ). So δ = 0.1 counts the rank-one model as a tenth of an observation of each entry, and δ = 1 weights it equally with the data; equivalently, δ μ[i,j] is a pseudocount proportional to the entry's expected count. Each variance estimate moves toward μ[i,j] by the fraction δ / (1 + δ), which biases it by δ (μ[i,j] - X[i,j]) / (1 + δ). At δ = 0 the result is biwhitening; as δ → ∞ the products (a[i] b[j])^2 tend to μ[i,j], the normalization used by the analytic Pearson residuals of Lause, Berens & Kobak (2021), which equalizes the noise variances only when the expected counts have rank one. δ trades the existence of the scaling against this bias, and MatrixCovers does not choose it. Unlike shrink = 0, a positive shrink is not covariant under rescaling of V, because μ is not.

Some zeros are neither structural nor sampling zeros. In mass spectrometry, a zero often means that a signal fell below the detection limit: the value is censored, not observed. Neither treatment is correct for censored values, and noise_cover makes no attempt to model them.

The Dyson equalizer of Landa & Kluger (2025) takes a different route: it computes the scaling from the singular values of the data, needs no variance estimate, and has no comparable existence condition.

Using the result

The examples below assume

using MatrixCovers, LinearAlgebra

a, b, stats = noise_cover(V)
Yhat = Diagonal(pinv.(a)) * Y * Diagonal(pinv.(b))
m, n = count(!iszero, a), count(!iszero, b)

Rank. Pure noise with unit variance has singular values at most about √m + √n, so the number of singular values of Yhat above that threshold estimates the rank of the signal:

σ = svdvals(Matrix(Yhat))   # or a truncated SVD for large sparse data
k = count(>(sqrt(m) + sqrt(n)), σ)

The threshold is asymptotic, so components just above or below it are uncertain. With shrink > 0, the total variance is unchanged, but its distribution among the entries moves toward the rank-one fit, so the noise variances are equalized only approximately.

Embedding and clustering. The leading singular vectors give an embedding of the rows or columns in units of the noise:

F = svd(Matrix(Yhat))
Zrows = F.U[:, 1:k] .* F.S[1:k]'   # one row per row of Y
Zcols = F.V[:, 1:k] .* F.S[1:k]'   # one row per column of Y

Euclidean distances in this embedding are measured in units of the noise. The noise contributes roughly k to the squared norm of each embedded unit, so a unit whose squared norm is not well above k cannot be distinguished from noise.

When units of the same type differ only in an overall gain, for example cells of one type that differ in brightness or in sequencing depth, their embedded vectors differ only in length. Normalizing each to unit length removes the gain before clustering:

Zc = Zcols ./ norm.(eachrow(Zcols))

This removes a pure gain and nothing else. If units of the same type also differ in sensitivity, for example in the concentration at which they respond, or if the measurement saturates for the brightest units, their vectors differ in direction as well. The type then occupies more than one dimension of the embedding, the rank estimate exceeds the number of types, and a clustering method that allows each type to span a subspace is more appropriate.

Returning to the original units. The scales are part of the result. Multiplying an estimate made from Yhat by the scales returns it to the units of Y. For example, a rank-k estimate of the signal is

Lhat = F.U[:, 1:k] * Diagonal(F.S[1:k]) * F.V[:, 1:k]'
Xhat = Diagonal(a) * Lhat * Diagonal(b)

and rows or columns with a zero scale are zero in Xhat. Quantities whose shape matters, such as a dose–response curve along the rows for responses to one ligand at several concentrations, should be computed from Xhat rather than from Yhat: each row of Yhat carries its own scale 1/a[i], which changes the shape of such a curve while leaving comparisons between columns unaffected. Do not try to make these scales equal across related rows; rows measured at different intensities or with different numbers of repeats have different noise, and equalizing the noise requires different scales.

For omics count data, BiPCA (Stanley et al., 2025) provides a complete pipeline built on biwhitening, including the choice of the variance model, rank estimation, and optimal shrinkage of the singular values.
